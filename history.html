Digital Accessibility

Accessible History Instruction

Make primary sources, archives, timelines, maps, and historical interpretation accessible.

Module Overview

Make primary sources, archives, timelines, maps, and historical interpretation accessible.

Digital accessibility helps students engage with the discipline through multiple usable formats and methods. Plan access to source materials, software, activities, and assessment from the start, while preserving the essential learning goals.

Learning Objectives

  • Identify accessibility barriers in historical documents, maps, and archival images.
  • Create accessible transcriptions and descriptions of primary sources.
  • Provide equivalent ways to explore timelines, geographic changes, and evidence.
  • Build inclusive research, discussion, and assessment activities.

How to Make History Materials More Accessible

Primary Sources and Archival Documents

Historical documents may be handwritten, degraded, or scanned as images.

  • Provide accurate transcriptions alongside scans; mark illegible words clearly rather than guessing.
  • Describe layout, marginalia, stamps, seals, handwriting, and physical condition when relevant.
  • Identify provenance, date, creator, and collection context.
  • Check reading order and text recognition when providing PDFs.
Historical Photographs and Artifacts

Descriptions should support historical inquiry rather than merely listing objects.

  • Identify subjects, setting, relationships, visible text, and relevant material details.
  • Distinguish observation from interpretation and acknowledge uncertainty.
  • Supply long descriptions when evidence depends on small or complex details.
Maps and Geographic Change

Historical maps combine symbols, boundaries, labels, and geographic relationships.

  • Explain scale, orientation, legend, and what a map represents.
  • Describe significant routes, borders, movement, and spatial changes.
  • Provide accessible tables of places, dates, or locations as alternatives to visual-only maps.
Timelines and Chronology

A visual timeline should not be the only way to understand sequence or overlap.

  • Provide a chronological list or structured table with dates and descriptions.
  • Explain simultaneity, durations, and cause-and-effect claims in text.
  • Avoid encoding historical periods by color alone.
Digitized Archives and Research Tools

Evaluate access to the actual search, filter, download, and reading workflow.

  • Check keyboard navigation, search labels, document viewers, and text alternatives.
  • Provide stable citations and accessible download formats when possible.
  • Offer an alternative path when a repository viewer is inaccessible.
Oral Histories and Recorded Sources

Audio and video interviews need equivalent access to their content.

  • Provide accurate transcripts with speaker identification and timestamps when helpful.
  • Caption video and describe important visual context.
  • Respect source attribution, permissions, and sensitive historical context.
Data, Charts, and Quantitative History

Statistical figures require textual interpretation and accessible data.

  • Identify units, populations, time periods, axes, and limitations.
  • Provide structured data tables and describe patterns and comparisons.
  • Avoid color-only encoding and explain uncertainty.
Inclusive Discussion and Assessment

Allow students to work with evidence without depending on one inaccessible medium.

  • Provide readable prompts and primary sources ahead of time.
  • Offer accessible options for essays, annotated timelines, exhibits, or presentations.
  • Assess evidence-based reasoning using consistent criteria.

Key Takeaways

  • Transcriptions complement scans; descriptions communicate material and historical context.
  • Represent maps and timelines through structured text or data as well as graphics.
  • Review archive interfaces and final student-facing formats for accessibility.
