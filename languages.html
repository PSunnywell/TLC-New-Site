Digital Accessibility

Accessible Foreign Language Instruction

Make language learning, pronunciation, writing systems, media, and conversation activities accessible.

Overview

Make language learning, pronunciation, writing systems, media, and conversation activities accessible.

Digital accessibility helps students engage with the discipline through multiple usable formats and methods. Plan access to source materials, software, activities, and assessment from the start, while preserving the essential learning goals.

How to Make Foreign Languages Materials More Accessible

Language Identification and Multilingual Documents

Assistive technology depends on correct language information for pronunciation and reading.

  • Set the document or page language and mark passages in other languages with the appropriate language code.
  • Use Unicode text rather than images of words whenever possible.
  • Avoid inserting spaces between letters to simulate formatting.
Writing Systems, Scripts, and Diacritics

Learners may need to distinguish characters, accents, directionality, and script conventions.

  • Use readable fonts that support the script and allow text enlargement.
  • Explain diacritics and unfamiliar characters in words, not color alone.
  • Preserve correct right-to-left direction for relevant scripts.
  • Provide text alternatives for handwritten examples and calligraphy.
Pronunciation and Phonetics

Pronunciation demonstrations should be supported by multiple forms of explanation.

  • Pair audio with written examples, IPA or other course-appropriate notation, and articulatory descriptions.
  • Identify stress, tone, rhythm, or contrasts important to the learning goal.
  • Do not assume one text spelling communicates pronunciation across languages.
Listening Comprehension and Media

Listening tasks may require individualized accommodations depending on the skill being assessed.

  • Provide accessible player controls, clear audio, and captions or transcripts when appropriate to the learning objective.
  • Caption spoken instructions and essential contextual video information.
  • Coordinate assessment accommodations without inadvertently changing the target skill.
Conversation and Speaking Activities

Make turn-taking and communication expectations explicit.

  • Provide discussion prompts, vocabulary, and interaction rules in advance.
  • Allow accessible communication tools and alternative participation methods where appropriate.
  • Use clear speaker identification in group activities.
Reading, Grammar, and Vocabulary

Structure supports navigation and comprehension in language materials.

  • Use real headings, lists, and tables with headers.
  • Avoid color-only grammatical markup; pair highlighting with labels or symbols.
  • Use descriptive links and accessible flashcards.
Translation, Subtitles, and Cultural Context

Translations and subtitles can support access, but are not always equivalent to the original language task.

  • Label original and translated text clearly.
  • Explain idioms, cultural references, and context when essential.
  • Provide text alternatives for images, maps, and culturally specific visual material.
Language-Learning Platforms and Assessments

Check whether learners can complete the exact activity using keyboard and assistive technology.

  • Test drag-and-drop, matching, timed quizzes, recording tools, and feedback messages.
  • Offer equivalent input methods for inaccessible interactions.
  • Check accessibility of the final files and coordinate individualized accommodations.

Key Takeaways

  • Mark changes in language correctly and use real Unicode text.
  • Offer multiple ways to access pronunciation, scripts, and cultural information.
  • Keep the assessed language skill clear when choosing accessibility supports and accommodations.
